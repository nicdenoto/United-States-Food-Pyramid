# "Taking the Russo-Williamson Thesis Seriously in the Social Sciences" — Virginia Ghiara (2022), *Synthese*

## Core thesis (RWT)
A causal claim can be established only if you establish both (a) a **difference-making relationship** between cause and effect, and (b) a **mechanism** linking them that is responsible for that difference-making relationship (Russo & Williamson 2007). Originally developed for the health sciences; recently extended to the social sciences (Shan & Williamson 2021).

**Ghiara's goal:** Defend RWT against two main criticisms and argue social scientists should endorse it, in both its *descriptive* (this is what scientists do) and *normative* (this is what scientists should do) forms.

Key nuance: establishing causation is **fallible**. Combining the two evidence types doesn't guarantee correctness — it *reduces the probability* of inferential mistakes / methodological bias (Illari 2011).

## Two kinds of evidence (Sect. 2)
- **Difference-making** evidence: shows the effect varies with the cause (probabilistic or counterfactual). Usually from RCTs, experiments, statistical analyses.
- **Mechanistic** evidence: shows a mechanism/process links cause and effect. Two philosophical lineages: (1) spatio-temporal physical process à la Salmon/Dowe (energy/momentum); (2) organized system of entities + activities à la Machamer/Craver/Glennan.
- **Crucial distinction:** *types of evidence* ≠ *types of evidence-gathering methods*. Mechanistic evidence need not come from "mechanistic" methods (e.g., statistical biomarker studies in epidemiology can yield mechanistic evidence — "meet-in-the-middle" approach). What matters is the *object* of evidence, not the method. (Fig. 1 formalizes this.)

## RWT in the social sciences (Sect. 3)
- Difference-making evidence works much as in health sciences.
- **Social mechanisms** are conceptualized heterogeneously; two broad camps: (1) **entities + activities** that generate the outcome (e.g., Schelling's segregation model); (2) **processes/chains of events** linking cause and effect through time/space (e.g., Skocpol's comparative study of revolutions in France/China/Russia).
- **Mixed methods research (3.2):** Many mixed-methods studies already do what RWT prescribes (qualitative → mechanisms, quantitative → difference-making), even if "evidential pluralism" isn't named explicitly. Shan & Williamson argue RWT gives mixed methods a *normative* justification the paradigms (positivism, etc.) don't. Caveat (via Crasnow): RWT is purely epistemological, offers no metaphysical foundations; metaphysical tensions still need addressing.
- **Process tracing (3.3):** A key method for gathering mechanistic evidence; definitions split along the same two mechanism concepts. Quantitative methods can also contribute ("pattern evidence," Beach & Pedersen).
- **Worked examples (3.4):** natural resources → civil war (Ross 2004); abortion legalization → falling crime (Donohue & Levitt); economic deprivation → child outcomes; Mexico–US migration (Garip & Asad 2016).

## Criticism 1 — Descriptive: "Social scientists don't use RWT" (Claveau 2012)
- **Claim:** In an unemployment case study, one causal claim (benefit-eligibility strictness reduces unemployment) was established via mechanism *only*, no difference-making evidence — because no cross-country-comparable measure existed. Claveau defends **causal monism** (all causation is difference-making/counterfactual-manipulationist); mechanistic evidence is just a fallback when good difference-making studies are hard.
- **Ghiara's defenses (three):**
  1. Mechanistic studies were good enough to establish *both* mechanism and (an unmeasured) difference-making relationship — combined with some RCT evidence.
  2. Researchers didn't establish causation, only that strictness is *likely* to reduce unemployment (hedged language in the cited review supports this).
  3. Even if it's a genuine exception, exceptions don't threaten RWT — Williamson (2018) already says RWT holds *normally*, allowing exceptions.
  - Against Claveau's monism: the rise of mixed methods and process tracing shows social scientists *do* treat mechanisms as constitutive of causation, not merely as a proxy for difference-making.

## Criticism 2 — Normative: "RWT doesn't work" (Reiss 2009)
- **Two parts:**
  - **(a) Masking example:** Violent TV → violence. A mechanism exists, and a correlation exists — but the correlation is spurious (common cause), because a second, counteracting mechanism masks the real one. So combining both evidence types can still mislead.
  - **(b) Causal pluralism:** "Cause" has multiple non-overlapping meanings; different evidence types answer different causal questions, so you can't combine them for a single claim. Also: omissions (e.g., negligence in law) cause effects with no physical mechanism.
- **Ghiara's defenses:**
  1. In the masking case, the difference-making relationship was **not properly established** — researchers failed to check dependence *conditional on confounders* (Williamson 2018). So the error violates RWT rather than refuting it.
  2. RWT is explicitly about **reducing bias**, not eliminating all error. Fallibility is built in, so real-life inferential mistakes aren't a counterexample.
  3. Against pluralism: social scientists broadly reject "X causes Y = mere correlation" (Elster, George & Bennett, Elsenbroich, Friedrichs all cited). The masking phenomenon itself shows scientists *won't* accept a mechanism as causal until masking is ruled out — i.e., they treat mechanism + difference-making as jointly necessary, consistent with RWT.

## Sect. 5 — Overcoming pluralist vs. monist accounts
- **Pluralism** (Reiss): each evidence type tied to its own concept of cause. **Monism** (Claveau): one concept (difference-making) for all.
- Ghiara: both fail to fit social-science practice, where scientists explicitly collect *both* evidence types for a *single* claim.
- Important: RWT is an account of the **epistemology** of causation and stays **neutral on metaphysics** — it doesn't dictate what concept of cause researchers must hold, so various metaphysical accounts remain compatible.

## Sect. 6–7 — Conclusion
- RWT applies in both descriptive and normative forms. Its normative value: it discriminates strong from weak causal knowledge and helps guard against confounding, overfitting, and masking.
- **Final caveat:** RWT can't guarantee success — establishing causation is fallible and good evidence is hard to collect — but it reduces inferential mistakes and structures causal research.
