# Draft — Introduction and Background

---

There has been longstanding debate, within the fields of epidemiology and public health policy, over the proper structuring of an evidence hierarchy: over which forms of evidence — clinical trial, mechanistic explanation, expert opinion, observational cohort — are sufficient, individually or in combination, to license a causal claim.¹ The stakes of this debate are not merely academic. When organizations and governments responsible for public health policy issue recommendations, they do so on a scale and with an authority that most scientific claims never carry; a recommendation that reaches a national population functions less like a hypothesis to be revised in the literature and more like a directive to be acted upon at the level of individual diet and behavior. This is what distinguishes public health guidance from other domains of scientific uncertainty: in most fields, an underdetermined or subsequently reversed conclusion costs the field time and credibility, but in public health, an underdetermined conclusion that is nonetheless promoted with confidence can cost individuals their health. Organizations responsible for health governance should therefore be held to a high standard of evidential confidence — a standard commensurate not with how confident the evidence happens to make researchers feel, but with how well the evidence structure underlying a claim actually supports the certainty with which that claim is issued.

The history of public health recommendations offers no shortage of cases in which this standard was not met, and in which the eventual reversal of a recommendation reveals a gap between the confidence with which it was issued and the evidence structure that in fact supported it. The supplementation of hormone replacement therapy (HRT) for menopausal women is among the clearest examples of this pattern.² Clinical findings in the 1970s linking HRT to increased cancer risk led to the dismantling of physician promotion of the therapy, until the addition of a progestin (e.g., medroxyprogesterone acetate) to treatment regimens was understood to minimize that risk and confidence in HRT was restored. A 2002 randomized trial, the Women's Health Initiative, again linked HRT to increased cancer risk, blood clots, and stroke, and supplementation rates fell accordingly — until its generalizability was challenged, chiefly under the "timing hypothesis," since its participants averaged 63 years of age and were often a decade or more past menopause, unlike the recently menopausal women in whom therapy is typically begun [T2] — and medical consensus shifted back toward regarding HRT as a beneficial intervention for many women.³ The result, decades on, is that patients and physicians alike are left searching for direction on a question that should, in principle, have a stable evidentiary answer; the ambiguity is not a failure of any single study so much as a failure to have demanded, from the outset, an evidence structure equal to the weight the recommendation carried.

A comparable pattern appears in the case of beta-carotene supplementation, where observational associations between beta-carotene intake and reduced cancer risk supported early recommendations, notwithstanding a biological mechanism that, once elucidated after the trials, pointed in the opposite direction of the observed association [T2]; the recommendation was later reversed when randomized evidence [T2 — trial identity and results to be confirmed against primary sources before citing] failed to replicate the protective effect and in some populations suggested harm.⁴ Vitamin E supplementation followed a similar arc, with observational cohort evidence of cardiovascular benefit failing to hold up under randomized controlled testing [T2 — specific trial citations still needed; flagging rather than asserting a source here per the provenance rule].⁵ Taken together with the HRT case, these three episodes are treated in this paper not as isolated errors but as a class: reversals in which an evidence structure leaning heavily on one leg — usually the observational, difference-making leg, and once, in the case of beta-carotene, contradicted at the mechanistic leg once that leg was properly investigated — was nonetheless treated by policy and clinical practice as sufficient to support a confident, population-scale recommendation.

This is, at root, a problem of causal inference under public pressure to decide. Epidemiology has long been attentive to the distinction between correlation and causation, and to the methods — adjustment for confounders, quasi-experimental design, sensitivity analysis — available to narrow the gap between the two.⁶ But this attentiveness sits uneasily against the practical demands of public health policy, which cannot simply decline to issue dietary or clinical guidance until causal uncertainty is fully resolved; a government agency is asked to recommend or restrict specific foods and interventions on a timeline set by public need, not by the pace at which the evidence matures. The Russo-Williamson Thesis (RWT) offers one influential answer to how causal claims might be responsibly established under this pressure, requiring that a causal claim be supported by both difference-making evidence — that the outcome varies with the purported cause — and mechanistic evidence — that a plausible mechanism links the two — rather than by either alone.⁷ The thesis is attractive precisely because it addresses the failure mode visible in the reversal cases above: a difference-making association, however statistically robust, that is never checked against a mechanistic account is vulnerable to being an artifact of confounding, while a mechanistic account that is never checked against difference-making evidence is vulnerable to being wrong about how the mechanism actually behaves in a target population.⁸ Solely mechanistic evidence carries its own characteristic failure, moreover, in the form of masking, whereby a second, concurrent mechanism can produce effects that counteract or obscure the first when the relevant variable is manipulated or measured — meaning that mechanism alone is no more sufficient a foundation for a causal claim than difference-making evidence alone.⁹ Quantitative bias analysis and the E-value offer complementary tools for this same problem from the difference-making side, providing a way to estimate how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away an observed association, and thereby giving some purchase on how much weight a given piece of difference-making evidence can actually bear.¹⁰

This paper proceeds from the premise that the question worth asking of a public health guidance document is not simply whether its recommendations are correct, but whether the certainty with which they are asserted matches the evidence structure that actually supports them — whether, in other words, an organization's chain of authority from evidence to recommendation is proportionate at every link, or whether it borrows certainty from one part of the evidence base to cover a gap in another.
