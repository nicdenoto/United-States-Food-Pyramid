## Corpus selection

An organization or agency's public health claims were judged eligible for this pipeline against four criteria: that the reasoning behind a recommendation be public and accessible, referencing mechanisms, clinical trials, or expert opinion rather than asserted without support; that the organization's impact be widespread and severe enough that an error in its recommendations carries real population-level consequence; that its references be documentable, organized, and efficiently retrievable, in the form of DOIs, PMIDs, or downloadable tables rather than informal citation; and that its recommendations be issued as firm, confident claims rather than tentative or exploratory ones, since it is precisely the confidence of a claim that this pipeline is built to interrogate against its evidence structure. The Scientific Foundation for the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025–2030, together with its appendices, satisfies all four criteria at a scale few other single documents do, and was accordingly fixed as this paper's primary corpus rather than treated as one candidate among an open set of organizations.

## The front-end/back-end distinction

The DGA document is best understood as having two layers, and the pipeline is built around auditing both rather than either alone. The front end — the main report — functions as an index of claims and their associated certainty labels; it is the layer a policymaker or member of the public is most likely to read, and the layer at which a recommendation's confidence is actually communicated. The back end — the appendices — contains the evidence structure that is supposed to support those certainty labels: the GRADE ratings, the cited trials and cohort studies, the mechanistic discussion. A recommendation can fail at either layer independently of the other; a front-end certainty label can overstate what its own back-end evidence review supports, and, in at least one case in this corpus, a food group's recommendation can appear in the front end with no dedicated back-end evidence review at all. The pipeline therefore treats claim extraction and evidence-hierarchy analysis as two coordinated but distinct passes over the document, rather than a single read-through.

## Claim and reference extraction

Claims are identified by human review of the front-end recommendations — this step is deliberately not automated, since determining what a document is actually claiming, as opposed to what it merely discusses, still requires judgment that the pipeline does not delegate to a model. Once a claim is identified, its supporting references, and the sub-references of those references in turn, are collected and converted to Markdown for storage in an organized corpus, preserving both the reference itself and enough surrounding context to re-derive how it was used. This collection step runs on a custom script and LLM assistance in combination, rather than either exclusively.

## Evidence-hierarchy analysis

With the claim and its reference chain assembled, an LLM is used, via a custom prompt, to analyze the claim and its references and reconstruct the chain of authority behind it: which categories of evidence the claim actually relies upon — clinical trial, mechanistic explanation, expert opinion, systematic review or meta-analysis, observational cohort — and to what severity it relies on each, as opposed to merely gesturing toward a category of evidence without substantively depending on it. The sub-references are analyzed in turn for their contribution to, and adequacy in supporting, the primary references, using the same appraisal apparatus applied to the primary claim: RWT as the governing lens, with GRADE, the Bradford Hill criteria, the E-value, quantitative bias analysis, and, where design-specific evaluation is warranted, ROBIS, AMSTAR-2, RoB 2, ROBINS-I, STROBE, and CONSORT as supporting instruments. Because an LLM's output is only as trustworthy as its calibration against correct human application of these criteria, the model's application of each is checked against worked, gold-standard examples of that criterion's correct use before its judgments on the corpus are relied upon.

## RWT as the operationalizing lens

RWT is not simply one entry in the supporting-apparatus list above; it is the principle that dictates what the evidence-hierarchy analysis is actually checking for. For every claim, the LLM-assisted chain-of-authority reconstruction has to resolve two questions separately, and never conflate them: what difference-making evidence supports the claim, and how strong it remains once adjusted for confounding via quantitative bias analysis, the E-value, and the Bradford Hill criteria; and, independently, what mechanistic evidence supports the claim, and whether that mechanistic account is complete enough to rule out masking — a second, concurrent mechanism producing an effect that counteracts or obscures the first. A claim's asserted certainty is judged proportionate only when both legs are resolved to a level consistent with that certainty, following RWT's requirement that a causal claim needs both legs, not either in isolation; where one leg is missing, weak, or has been assumed rather than established, the claim is flagged as under-supported regardless of how strong the other leg happens to be. This is what turns RWT from a citation in the literature review into an operating rule of the pipeline itself: the whole-grains case, where the RCT record supports mechanism more convincingly than it supports difference-making, and the saturated-fat case, positioned as a reversal performed in real time rather than retrospectively, are both direct applications of this two-leg check, not illustrations appended to the methodology after the fact.

## LLM calibration: justification, parameters, replicability

Because the evidence-hierarchy analysis and the RWT dual-leg check above are performed with LLM assistance, the pipeline's credibility depends on being able to state, and defend, how the model was calibrated before its outputs on the DGA corpus were trusted. This is documented under three headings, after the hypothesis the calibration work is designed to test.

**Hypothesis, evaluated alongside the DGA audit.** *"When calibrated through iterative refinement against published gold-standard examples, an LLM can correctly apply established evidentiary-appraisal frameworks (GRADE, the Bradford Hill criteria, the E-value, and quantitative bias analysis) to a body of evidence and reach verdicts that match expert human application of those same frameworks."* This is a hypothesis the calibration protocol is designed to test, not a capability this paper asserts. It is run alongside the DGA evidence-structure audit rather than in place of it, and the audit remains the paper's primary purpose whatever the calibration work concludes. The hypothesis is scoped to framework application only; sourcing and reference collection (Stage 2) are not calibrated against gold-standard examples and are not claimed here.

**Justification.** [DRAFT — grounded in an observation already recorded for this project; confirm the framing before treating it as final.] The claims this pipeline asks the LLM to adjudicate do not admit the kind of mechanical check a code output does, where an incorrect result visibly fails; an incorrect evidentiary judgment instead reads as a plausible one. An earlier phase of this project, working with LLM-assisted code generation, saw correction rates decline across successive research-log entries; that decline reflects the shift from a verifiable task, where a wrong output announces itself, to an unverifiable one, where it does not — not necessarily an improvement in the model's underlying reasoning. This is precisely the failure mode calibration is built to guard against: before the model's chain-of-authority reconstructions, GRADE ratings, or RWT assessments on the DGA corpus are relied upon, its outputs on a fixed set of worked examples with a known-correct human application of the relevant criterion are checked against that known answer, so that confidence in the model's judgment rests on demonstrated agreement rather than on the fluency of its prose.

**Parameters.** Calibration covers four frameworks: GRADE, the Bradford Hill criteria, the E-value, and quantitative bias analysis. Gold-standard examples are drawn from published methodological sources rather than self-authored from this project's own corpus, so the model is never calibrated against a claim it is later judged on. Each framework's set combines positive examples, which are published, correct applications of the framework, with one negative example, a published misapplication paired with its diagnosis. For GRADE, the positive examples are Chapter 14 of the Cochrane Handbook, the GRADEpro Handbook, and Guyatt et al. (2011). For the Bradford Hill criteria, they are Hill (1965), Fedak et al. (2015), and an applied assessment of Zika virus and microcephaly. For the E-value, they are VanderWeele and Ding (2017), VanderWeele, Ding and Mathur (2019), and Linden, Mathur and VanderWeele (2020). For quantitative bias analysis, they are Barberio et al. (2021), which covers unmeasured confounding; Bokern et al. (2025), which covers outcome misclassification; and Barnett et al. (2017), which covers selection bias in a cluster randomised trial. The negative examples are a conflict-of-interest vignette from Granholm et al. (2024) for GRADE; Mente et al.'s (2009) reduction of Hill's viewpoints to a summed checklist for Bradford Hill; Poole's (2020) worked non-significance E-value for the E-value; and Lash et al.'s (2021) re-analysis of a misreported bias parameter for quantitative bias analysis. Every negative example was required to meet four criteria: it had to be bounded to a single named case, citable, built around a specifically diagnosable flaw, and paired with a known correct counterfactual. Each framework is scored against its own rubric, cross-checked against that framework's sources. A rubric dimension is scored Match only when the model's judgment and its reasoning both agree with the gold standard, and a Partial is treated as a failure. Calibration runs in a separate Claude account that a human operator configures by hand before the first run, using Claude Sonnet 5 at medium reasoning effort. Web search, reference to past conversations, memory, and network access are disabled in that account, and code execution is enabled. The pipeline does not configure its own environment. The calibration prompt for each framework is placed in that Project's instructions field, and the model's context contains nothing else except the single source attached to the conversation at hand. Rubrics, interpretation notes, and answer keys stay with the human scorer and are never loaded into the model's context. The prompt itself is not fixed in advance; it is discovered through the refinement protocol below and then locked.

**Replicability.** For a pipeline making causal-evidentiary judgments under faculty supervision, replicability is part of the argument that its outputs can be trusted at all, not a courtesy appended afterward. This requires versioning and preserving, alongside the corpus itself: the calibration example set, so a later reviewer can see exactly what the model was checked against; the exact prompts used at each pipeline stage, since a rephrased prompt is a different, unvalidated instrument even when the underlying criterion is unchanged; the model identifier and version, since calibration performed against one version does not transfer to another; and a research log of every human correction made during calibration or live analysis, capturing not just that a correction occurred but what the model got wrong and why. The Week 2–3 AI-correction transcripts already flagged elsewhere in this project as high-priority evidentiary material are exactly this record, and belong here as the documented basis for this replicability requirement.

Calibration is an iterative refinement procedure, not a one-time agreement score measured against a fixed threshold. Each revision of a framework's prompt is tested in its own new, isolated Project, named `<Framework>Application<N>`, and is never edited and re-run inside an existing one. Isolation therefore comes from starting a clean Project for every revision. A revision must pass three gates in sequence:

- **Lock-in.** Run against the positive examples, the revision must score Match on every applicable rubric dimension for every worked example.
- **Misuse detection.** Given the negative example, the revision must correctly diagnose the specific flaw and state what a correct application would have concluded.
- **Non-retrieval.** Because every published source states its own conclusion, a revision could pass the first two gates by copying an answer it had read rather than deriving one. It is therefore run once more on a constructed case that has never been published, whose answer is computed independently and never shown to the model.

A failure at either of the first two gates sends the prompt back for a revision aimed at the dimension that diverged. A failure at the third gate is treated as more serious: it suggests the earlier passes may themselves reflect pattern-matching, so the whole cycle restarts. Only a revision that clears all three gates becomes that framework's `<Framework>Frozen` Project, the versioned artifact carried into analysis of the DGA corpus. The framework is then run once against a held-out, non-circular nutrition-domain application of it, as a domain-transfer check before it touches the corpus. That check is Popoff et al. (2019) for GRADE, Hill et al. (2022) for Bradford Hill, Robinson and Jones (2024) for the E-value, and Pakzad et al. (2023) for quantitative bias analysis. The frozen prompt is not rewritten on the basis of the transfer check; a mismatch there is reported as a limitation. ## Provenance discipline and the burden-of-proof check

Every claim carried through the pipeline is tagged at one of three provenance tiers: verified directly against a primary document, sourced from a secondary reference or search and not yet corpus-verified, or inferred from the structure of the argument rather than stated outright. Conflating these tiers is treated as a methodological error in its own right, independent of whether the underlying claim later turns out to be correct. This discipline supports the pipeline's central analytical move, which is to apply the report's own evidentiary standard symmetrically: the DGA appendices apply a directional standard of evidence when supporting their preferred recommendations, and this pipeline applies that same standard back to those recommendations rather than granting them a presumption the appendices do not extend to competing evidence.

## From diagnosis to construction

Where the evidence-hierarchy analysis identifies a recommendation whose asserted certainty outruns its evidence structure — an unaddressed reliance on a single evidential leg, or a mechanism acknowledged elsewhere in the document but never applied back to the recommendation in question — the pipeline's aim is not to stop at naming the gap. Existing contrary evidence, mechanistic or anthropological, is deliberately sought out and supplied as a substitute for the missing leg, so that each case analysis in this paper ends with a proposed evidentiary correction rather than only a critique.
